Abstract
India’s data centre build-out is accelerating into states whose energy grids burn more than 70% coal
and whose groundwater boards have already declared aquifer over-exploitation. Without quantitative
tipping-point analysis, planners cannot distinguish a viable siting decision from a commitment to
irreversible resource depletion. This pipeline addresses that gap for four candidate host states-Telangana,
Andhra Pradesh, Gujarat, and West Bengal-by integrating air quality, groundwater, electricity, and
renewable capacity data into a unified analytical framework.
The system trains seven model architectures across ten modules: a weighted composite readiness scorer,
a monotonically constrained XGBoost air quality forecaster, a lag-featured Random Forest groundwater
regressor, a Bayesian Ridge pollution model, ARIMA and Holt-Winters renewable capacity forecasters,
a K-Means national clustering model, and a district-level groundwater regressor. Causal effects are
estimated via a directed acyclic graph with backdoor adjustment and a difference-in-differences routine.
The pipeline produces approximately 35 CSV artifacts and 40 figures per run, culminating in state-level
readiness rankings and groundwater tipping-point thresholds under capacity multipliers from 0.5× to
3.0×.
The most severe finding is structural: the preprocessing module is not orchestrated within the main
pipeline runner, meaning model outputs can silently lag behind updated source data. Integrating
preprocessing as a gated Stage 0 is the single highest-priority remediation before this framework is
used to inform policy allocation decisions.
Keywords: machine learning, data engineering, causal inference, environmental analytics, pipeline
architecture.

Introduction
Deploying large-scale AI data centres in India commits a region to a specific trajectory of resource
depletion. A single hyperscale facility at 500 MW draws power continuously, consumes millions of litres
of water daily for cooling, and anchors its carbon footprint to the local generation mix. In states where
groundwater is already classified as over-exploited by the Central Ground Water Board and where coal
supplies more than 70% of installed capacity, adding permanent high-density compute loads is not a
neutral planning decision. Yet infrastructure readiness assessments have historically relied on qualitative
checklists and fragmented sector reports that cannot be reconciled or updated as conditions change. The
pipeline documented here addresses that gap directly for four candidate host states - Telangana, Andhra
Pradesh, Gujarat, and West Bengal - over a simulation horizon extending to 2035 [src:
config.py,
FORECAST_END_YEAR].
The pipeline operates as a sequence of twenty named modules, each with a defined input contract and
output artifact. Ingestion and standardisation run in catfish.py; targeted domain cleaning runs in
Data_processing/Cleaner.py; and quality auditing runs in Data_processing/DataChecker.py.
The modelling layer then proceeds in the order defined by run_all.py [src:
run_all.py,
scripts]: train_readiness computes the weighted composite readiness score; train_impact
calculates the deterministic environmental footprint; train_forecast projects renewable capacity to
2035; train_pollution fits a Bayesian Ridge regression on PM2.5; train_classifier attempts a
binary shortage classifier; train_aqi_regressor fits a monotonically constrained XGBoost model;
train_groundwater_regressor builds a Random Forest over lag-engineered groundwater depth;
train_energy_shortage_regressor regresses energy demand; train_national_models extends
readiness ranking to all Indian states via K-Means clustering; and train_district_regressor fits a
district-level groundwater extraction model. Ten analysis modules then cover district divergence, seasonal
stress, Monte Carlo rank stability, scenario simulation, renewable utilisation gaps, a master summary
merge, a metrics report, national ranking, research question answering, and causal inference via a directed
acyclic graph.
Every method, parameter, formula, and output described here is traceable to a specific line or function
in the inspected source files. No behaviour is inferred beyond what is explicitly and unconditionally
present in the executed code path. A code-grounded audit of this kind creates a falsifiable description
of the system - one that can be verified, challenged, and improved without requiring a live execution
environment.
Scope note. All claims in this report derive from static inspection of the source files listed in
Section 2.

Geographic Scope
The four primary states represent contrasting infrastructure profiles. Gujarat offers the highest renewable
penetration among the group; Telangana and West Bengal operate coal-dominant grids; Andhra Pradesh
occupies an intermediate position with active solar expansion plans. This contrast makes the cross-state
comparison analytically productive for policy that must differentiate between jurisdictions rather than
treating India as a uniform infrastructure landscape.

config.py, FORECAST_END_YEAR]. Historical ob-
servations span from approximately 2007, giving the time-series models a training window of fifteen to
eighteen years depending on data availability per state - sufficient to capture multiple monsoon cycles,
which are the dominant source of annual variance in groundwater recharge.

3.1
Preprocessing Pipeline Architecture
Preprocessing is architecturally separate from modelling for a principled reason: the same cleaned datasets
must underlie every trained model, and that guarantee can only be enforced if cleaning is a distinct, gated
step. When preprocessing is co-located with model training - or, as in this pipeline, executed manually and
separately from run_all.py - a stale-data risk arises. If the raw source files are updated but the cleaning
step is not re-run, all downstream models silently continue operating on data that no longer reflects
ground truth. The module catfish.py was designed to address the practical problem of ingesting files
with inconsistent column naming, inconsistent state name conventions, and varying missingness patterns
across independently produced government datasets [src:
catfish.py, ]. It applies a sequence of

3.1.1
Universal Normalisation Steps (apply_universal_steps())
1. Strip leading/trailing whitespace from all column names.
2. Normalise state column naming conventions.
3. Canonicalise state strings to a reference vocabulary defined in config.py.
Canonicalising state name strings is the single most consequential normalisation step in the pipeline.
Government datasets use heterogeneous string representations for the same administrative unit: “Andhra
Pradesh”, “andhra pradesh”, “A.P.”, and “A P” all refer to the same entity but would produce four
separate rows in a grouped aggregation and four separate NaN entries in a merge. Without canonicalisation,
a join between the AQI dataset and the groundwater dataset silently drops rows wherever state name
strings differ by even a single character, producing a merged dataset that appears structurally complete but
is missing entire state-years of data. The reference vocabulary in config.py provides the authoritative
string form, and all source datasets are mapped to it before any feature engineering or model training
proceeds.

3.3
Feature Engineering
The quality report’s orphaned status has a concrete operational consequence. If a source dataset contained
40% missing values in a key feature column - for example, groundwater_depth or pm25_mean - the
DataChecker would detect and log this condition, but no mechanism exists to halt or warn the training
pipeline. The affected model would train on a degraded feature matrix, producing fitted model metrics
that appear valid but rest on defective data. The remediation is to add a missingness gate in run_all.py
that reads the quality report JSON before invoking any training module and aborts with a descriptive error
if any feature column exceeds a configurable threshold - 20% is a reasonable default.

Statistical and Machine Learning Methods
Seven distinct model architectures are deployed across ten training modules, targeting five separate
environmental outcomes. Algorithm selection throughout reflects three practical constraints: state-level
datasets limited to four primary states, temporal data structures that require lag-aware train/test splitting,
and the need for interpretable outputs that can be communicated to non-technical policy stakeholders.

4.1
Readiness Model (train_readiness.py)
4.1.1
Rationale
A weighted composite index is appropriate for readiness scoring because infrastructure readiness is not
directly observable - it is a latent summary of four independently measurable dimensions: energy grid
capacity, water availability, renewable penetration, and carbon intensity. A composite score makes the
weighting structure explicit and auditable, whereas a supervised model trained on external readiness labels
would impose implicit weightings that cannot be inspected. The weights are sourced from config.py, so
they can be updated for different policy priorities without retraining. The PCA validation step confirms
that the composite score captures a real latent factor rather than an arbitrary numerical average [src:
train_readiness.py, run()].

4.2
AQI Regressor (train_aqi_regressor.py)
4.2.1
Rationale
XGBoost with monotonic constraints is the right choice for this AQI regression task. The PM2.5 target
exhibits non-linear relationships with its predictors - particularly renewable capacity and industrial
activity - and XGBoost handles these interactions natively through gradient-boosted trees. The monotonic
constraint on renewable capacity is the key implementation decision: it ensures the model cannot predict
decreasing PM2.5 as renewable capacity decreases, even if the small training dataset contains a spurious
counter-example. Without this constraint, an XGBoost model trained on four states would have enough
flexibility to learn noise from an 80-observation dataset and produce physically implausible predictions.
The source code does not document the rationale for this selection [src:
train_aqi_regressor.py,
], but the constraint itself makes the prediction surface defensible.

Groundwater Depth Regressor (train_groundwater_regressor.py)
Random Forest is the right algorithm for groundwater depth regression because the relationship between
depth to water level and its drivers - precipitation, extraction rate, seasonal cycle, and prior-year depth - is

5
Causal Analysis
Predictive modelling and causal inference answer fundamentally different questions. A regression model
can identify that high renewable capacity correlates with lower PM2.5 concentrations - it cannot establish
that increasing renewable capacity will cause PM2.5 to fall. The distinction matters because correlation
between two variables can arise from a shared upstream driver, from reverse causation, or from selection
effects, none of which imply that intervening on one variable will produce the expected change in the
other. The pipeline’s causal inference module causal_analysis.py answers the interventional question
directly: what would happen to regional air quality, groundwater stress, and energy shortage risk if a policy
deployed X MW of data centre capacity in state s? This is the question a policymaker, not a statistician,
needs answered [src:
causal_analysis.py, run()].
The directed acyclic graph (DAG) is the foundational object of the causal framework. It encodes the
researcher’s explicit assumptions about which variables cause which, through which pathways, and which
paths are blocked by conditioning on observed confounders. Without a DAG, covariate selection in causal
adjustment is arbitrary - a researcher choosing which variables to include in a regression has no principled
basis, and the resulting estimate may be biased upward, downward, or in sign depending on which
confounding paths remain open. With a DAG, variable selection follows a mechanical procedure: identify

Sensitivity Analysis
The sensitivity analysis systematically removes edges from the DAG to test estimator robustness [src:
causal_analysis.py, ]. By iteratively excluding candidate confounders and re-estimating the treat-
ment effect, the routine bounds the range of plausible causal estimates under different structural assump-
tions. Wide bounds indicate that the estimate depends heavily on specific edge inclusions; narrow bounds
indicate robustness to reasonable model uncertainty.
5.4
Threats to Causal Validity
Three structural threats limit the strength of causal identification in this pipeline.
1. Unmeasured confounding. The DAG cannot include variables that are not present in the dataset.
Industrial output, urbanisation rate, and infrastructure investment are plausible common causes of
both energy demand and air quality that are absent from the four-dataset corpus. Any such omitted
variable will partially confound the estimated treatment effects, biasing coefficients toward or away
from zero depending on the direction of the omitted correlation.
2. SUTVA violation. The stable unit treatment value assumption (SUTVA) requires that a state’s
potential outcomes are unaffected by which treatment other states receive. Indian state electricity

grids are interconnected through inter-state transmission agreements, and groundwater aquifers cross
administrative borders. A data centre added in Telangana may alter grid frequency in Andhra Pradesh
or reduce shared aquifer levels, violating SUTVA and rendering the estimated state-level effects
non-separable.
3. Absence of randomisation. Data centre siting decisions are not randomly assigned: states with better
infrastructure attract more investment, creating a selection-into-treatment bias. Without an instrument
or a natural experiment that induces exogenous variation in placement, the DiD estimate conflates the
causal effect of the data centre with pre-existing trends in high-readiness states. The parallel trends
diagnostic prescribed in Section 9 is a necessary but not sufficient check for this threat.

Probabilistic and Simulation Methods
The deterministic model outputs answer what is most likely under current trajectories. They do not answer
how sensitive those outputs are to the assumptions embedded in the analysis, or what happens under
plausible-but-not-most-likely alternative futures. Two simulation modules address these gaps: the Monte
Carlo stability module quantifies sensitivity to subjective weighting choices, and the scenario simulation
module maps the space of plausible infrastructure futures.

6.2
Scenario Stress Simulation
Point forecasts predict the most likely outcome under a single set of assumptions. Infrastructure planning
requires understanding the range of plausible futures: what happens if data centre growth is faster than
expected, if agricultural water demand increases concurrently, or if renewable deployment falls behind
target? Scenario simulation maps this uncertainty space by computing groundwater extraction percentage
under a discrete grid of capacity multipliers. Each multiplier produces a conditional future, enabling
policymakers to identify tipping points: the minimum capacity at which a state crosses from a safe
operating regime into a critical or over-exploited one.

Strengths and Weaknesses Analysis
A pipeline spanning data ingestion, unified cleaning, ten training modules, nine analysis modules, and a
serving layer inevitably involves design tradeoffs. The following assessment is grounded exclusively in
static code analysis: strengths and weaknesses are identified at the level of architecture and implementation.
Domain-specific modelling quality would require runtime data and domain expertise to evaluate fully.
Where a weakness is identified, a specific remediation path is given; where source code does not document
the rationale for a design decision, this is noted.
8.1
Methodological Strengths
1. Modular orchestration with explicit fault isolation. run_all.py wraps each run() call in a
try/except block and accumulates per-step status, preventing a single module failure from silently
corrupting downstream results. [src:
run_all.py, main()]
2. Centralised configuration eliminating magic numbers. All engineering coefficients, thresholds,
Monte Carlo parameters, and forecast horizons are defined as named constants in config.py, making
the parameter space auditable in a single file.
3. Uncertainty quantification via multiple mechanisms. The pipeline deploys three complementary
UQ strategies: Monte Carlo weight perturbation (Section 6.1), Bayesian Ridge posterior inference
(Section 4.4), and SHAP attribution (Section 4.2).
4. Causal framework embedded in the codebase. The presence of a DAG specification and DiD
routine in causal_analysis.py demonstrates methodological awareness of confounding, even if the
module is not yet orchestrated.
5. Structured cleaning log. catfish.py writes a cleaning_log.txt documenting which transfor-
mations were applied per source file, supporting audit trails.

8.4
External Validity Assessment
External validity refers to whether model predictions generalise beyond the training distribution - to
states not in the training data, to future periods beyond the forecast horizon, or to data collected under
different measurement protocols. The geographic scope is bounded by config.py [src:
config.py,
STATES]: predictions for states outside Telangana, Andhra Pradesh, Gujarat, and West Bengal are not
supported by the fitted models without retraining on a broader dataset. The forecast horizon of 2035
bounds temporal extrapolation; structural breaks in energy policy, monsoon patterns, or agricultural water
demand after that year are not captured by any model in the pipeline. The district-level groundwater
model faces an additional geographic sparsity limitation, as many districts have fewer than five annual
observations; coefficient estimates in these districts should be treated as indicative rather than reliable,
and any siting decision affecting a data-sparse district should draw on primary field survey data rather
than pipeline outputs alone.

Recommendations for Future Work
The six recommendations below are ordered by estimated implementation effort and impact on pipeline
reliability. Items 1-3 address correctness risks - stale data propagation, path failures, and metric drift
from hardcoded literals - that could silently invalidate results without any visible error. Items 4-6 address
completeness and scientific rigour: orchestration of the causal module, enforcement of a held-out test
partition, and formalisation of the parallel trends assumption. All six can be implemented without
architectural redesign.
1. Integrate preprocessing into run_all.py as Stage 0. Invoke catfish.py and Cleaner.py before
any training module, gated by an MD5/SHA-256 hash freshness check on raw input files. This
eliminates the stale-data propagation risk identified in Section 8.2.
2. Unify all file paths through config.py. Define a single canonical root (data/) and resolve all
downstream paths relative to it. Remove all hardcoded directory strings outside config.py. This
resolves the path-case duality risk on case-sensitive filesystems.
3. Replace hardcoded narrative constants with config.py imports. In metrics_report.py, re-
place literal strings such as “Simulations: 2000” with f"Simulations:
{config.MC_ITERATIONS}".
This ensures narrative statements remain consistent with actual parameter values across all future runs.
4. Orchestrate causal_analysis.py as an optional stage. Add a boolean flag RUN_CAUSAL to
config.py and conditionally invoke the causal module within run_all.py, ensuring its outputs are
reproducibly linked to the same data vintage as the predictive models.
5. Enforce a held-out test partition. Implement a global train/validation/test split at the data loading
stage, log split indices to a JSON artifact, and enforce that no feature engineering step uses test-

Conclusion
This report documented, formalised, and audited an end-to-end environmental impact assessment pipeline
for AI data centre deployment in four Indian states. The pipeline integrates four independent government
datasets - air quality, groundwater, electricity, and renewable capacity - through a unified preprocessing
layer, trains seven model architectures across ten training modules, runs nine analysis modules, and
serves all outputs through a nine-tab Streamlit dashboard. Over approximately 35 CSV artifacts and 40
figure files, it produces readiness scores, environmental impact calculations, time-series forecasts, causal
effect estimates, Monte Carlo rank stability distributions, scenario stress simulations, and a long-horizon
groundwater depletion projection to 2070. The resulting system is technically comprehensive in scope.
The methodological strengths are substantial. Modular orchestration in run_all.py with per-step
try/except isolation ensures a single module failure does not silently corrupt downstream results. Cen-
tralised config.py makes the full parameter space auditable in one file. Three complementary uncertainty
quantification mechanisms - Monte Carlo weight perturbation, Bayesian Ridge posterior inference, and
SHAP attribution - provide a richer characterisation of uncertainty than any single mechanism would alone.
The directed acyclic graph specification in causal_analysis.py demonstrates principled awareness of
confounding, going beyond the correlational analyses that dominate comparable environmental impact
assessments.
Two critical risks require resolution before the system is suitable for policy use. The preprocessing
orchestration gap — catfish.py not invoked by run_all.py — means a pipeline run following a data
update silently computes all outputs on a stale cleaned dataset. The path-case duality (Data/ versus
data/) creates portability failures on Linux and macOS case-sensitive filesystems. Either risk can silently
invalidate the entire pipeline’s output without producing a visible error. Both are remediable without
architectural redesign.
Three actions are necessary before production deployment: (1) integrate preprocessing into run_all.py
as a gated Stage 0; (2) generate a requirements.txt to pin the dependency environment; (3) enforce
a documented held-out test partition with logged split indices. These changes would raise the pipeline
from a research prototype to a system whose outputs can be defended in a policy or regulatory setting.
Code-grounded audits of this kind are a critical step in that transition: they surface the gap between what
a system appears to do and what the code actually does, producing a falsifiable description that can be
verified, challenged, and improved.
