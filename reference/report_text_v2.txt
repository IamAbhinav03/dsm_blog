Abstract
India’s data centre expansion is intersecting with two resource constraints that existing siting frame-
works do not adequately capture: electricity grids with high coal dependence and groundwater systems
already under stress in several high-growth states. This pipeline develops a quantitative analytical
framework for four candidate host states (Telangana, Andhra Pradesh, Gujarat, and West Bengal)
integrating air quality, groundwater, electricity, and renewable capacity data into a unified readiness
assessment.
The system trains seven model architectures across ten modules: a weighted composite readiness scorer,
a monotonically constrained XGBoost air quality forecaster, a lag-featured Random Forest groundwater
regressor, a Bayesian Ridge pollution model, ARIMA and Holt-Winters renewable capacity forecasters,
a K-Means national clustering model, and a district-level groundwater regressor. Causal effects are
estimated through a directed acyclic graph with backdoor adjustment and a difference-in-differences
routine. Each run produces approximately 35 CSV artifacts and 40 figures, culminating in state-level
readiness rankings and groundwater tipping-point thresholds evaluated under capacity multipliers from
0.5× to 3.0×.
Keywords: machine learning, data engineering, causal inference, environmental analytics, pipeline
architecture.

1
Introduction
Deploying large-scale AI data centres in India commits a region to a specific trajectory of resource
depletion. A single hyperscale facility at 500 MW draws power continuously, consumes millions of litres
of water daily for cooling, and anchors its carbon footprint to the local generation mix. In states where
groundwater is already classified as over-exploited by the Central Ground Water Board and where coal
supplies more than 70% of installed capacity, adding permanent high-density compute loads is not a
neutral planning decision. Existing infrastructure readiness assessments rely on qualitative checklists and
fragmented sector reports that cannot be reconciled as conditions change. The pipeline documented here
addresses that gap for four candidate host states, Telangana, Andhra Pradesh, Gujarat, and West Bengal,
over a simulation horizon extending to 2035 [src:
config.py, FORECAST_END_YEAR].
The pipeline operates as a sequence of twenty named modules, each with a defined input contract and
output artifact. Ingestion and standardisation run in catfish.py; targeted domain cleaning runs in
Data_processing/Cleaner.py; and quality auditing runs in Data_processing/DataChecker.py.
The modelling layer then proceeds in the order defined by run_all.py [src:
run_all.py,
scripts]: train_readiness computes the weighted composite readiness score; train_impact
calculates the deterministic environmental footprint; train_forecast projects renewable capacity to
2035; train_pollution fits a Bayesian Ridge regression on PM2.5; train_classifier attempts a
binary shortage classifier; train_aqi_regressor fits a monotonically constrained XGBoost model;
train_groundwater_regressor builds a Random Forest over lag-engineered groundwater depth;
train_energy_shortage_regressor regresses energy demand; train_national_models extends
readiness ranking to all Indian states via K-Means clustering; and train_district_regressor fits a
district-level groundwater extraction model. Ten analysis modules then cover district divergence, seasonal
stress, Monte Carlo rank stability, scenario simulation, renewable utilisation gaps, a master summary
merge, a metrics report, national ranking, research question answering, and causal inference via a directed
acyclic graph.
Every method, parameter, formula, and output described here is traceable to a specific line or function in
the source files. No behaviour is inferred beyond what is explicitly present in the executed code path.

Joining four independently produced government datasets into a single modelling pipeline is non-trivial.
AQI measurements originate from the Central Pollution Control Board monitoring network; groundwater
levels come from the Central Ground Water Board’s seasonal sampling programme; energy figures come
from the Central Electricity Authority state-wise generation reports; and renewable capacity comes from
Ministry of New and Renewable Energy project registrations. The datasets differ in temporal resolution
(annual versus seasonal versus monthly), geographic granularity (state versus district), unit systems
(microgrammes per cubic metre, metres below ground, million units, megawatts), and column naming
conventions. A naive join on state name strings would produce silent mismatches wherever naming strings
differ by capitalisation, spacing, or diacritics. The preprocessing module catfish.py was written to
resolve this heterogeneity, unifying all four sources under a common schema with canonicalised state
identifiers before any modelling step is invoked.
The geographic and temporal boundaries of the dataset impose hard limits on the pipeline’s inferential
scope. The primary state list contains Telangana, Andhra Pradesh, Gujarat, and West Bengal [src:
config.py, STATES], with Maharashtra also referenced in the filtering logic [src:
catfish.py,
TARGET_STATES]. All model training, feature engineering, and readiness scoring operates within this
state boundary. Predictions or rankings for states outside this set are not supported by the fitted models
and should not be extrapolated without retraining on a broader dataset. The forecast horizon extends to
2035 [src:
config.py, FORECAST_END_YEAR] - a window spanning the expected first and second
phases of India’s large-scale data centre build-out - and the pipeline cannot produce reliable estimates
beyond it without retraining on updated observations.
2.2
Geographic Scope
The four primary states represent contrasting infrastructure profiles. Gujarat offers the highest renewable
penetration among the group; Telangana and West Bengal operate coal-dominant grids; Andhra Pradesh
occupies an intermediate position with active solar expansion plans. This contrast makes the cross-state
comparison analytically productive for policy that must differentiate between jurisdictions rather than
treating India as a uniform infrastructure landscape.
2.3
Temporal Scope
The simulation horizon extends to 2035 [src:
config.py, FORECAST_END_YEAR]. Historical ob-
servations span from approximately 2007, giving the time-series models a training window of fifteen to
eighteen years depending on data availability per state - sufficient to capture multiple monsoon cycles,
which are the dominant source of annual variance in groundwater recharge.

Data Processing and Feature Engineering
The AIDER pipeline implements a multi-stage data processing and feature engineering workflow, trans-
forming raw heterogeneous inputs into a canonical, research-grade dataset suitable for predictive and
causal modelling.
3.1
Data Normalisation and Preprocessing
3.1.1
Universal Normalisation Steps (apply_universal_steps())
1. Strip leading/trailing whitespace from all column names.
2. Normalise state column naming conventions.
3. Canonicalise state strings to a reference vocabulary defined in config.py.
Canonicalising state name strings is the single most consequential normalisation step in the pipeline.
Government datasets use heterogeneous string representations for the same administrative unit: “Andhra
Pradesh”, “andhra pradesh”, “A.P.”, and “A P” all refer to the same entity but would produce four
separate rows in a grouped aggregation and four separate NaN entries in a merge. Without canonicalisation,
a join between the AQI dataset and the groundwater dataset silently drops rows wherever state name
strings differ by even a single character, producing a merged dataset that appears structurally complete but
is missing entire state-years of data. The reference vocabulary in config.py provides the authoritative
string form, and all source datasets are mapped to it before any feature engineering or model training
proceeds.

4
Statistical and Machine Learning Methods
This section details the diverse machine learning architectures and statistical models deployed in the
pipeline, ranging from composite scoring and time-series forecasting to supervised regression and national-
scale clustering.
Seven distinct model architectures are deployed across ten training modules, targeting five separate
environmental outcomes. Algorithm selection throughout reflects three practical constraints: state-level
datasets limited to four primary states, temporal data structures that require lag-aware train/test splitting,
and the need for interpretable outputs that can be communicated to non-technical policy stakeholders.
Each model is documented here under the WHY-HOW-WHAT structure: the motivation for the algorithm
choice, the exact implementation mechanics, and what the model’s output drives downstream.
4.1
Readiness Model (train_readiness.py)
The readiness module implements a multi-dimensional scoring framework that evaluates state-level
infrastructure across four critical environmental and resource axes.
4.1.1
Rationale
A weighted composite index is appropriate for readiness scoring because infrastructure readiness is not
directly observable; it is a latent summary of four independently measurable dimensions: energy grid
capacity, water availability, renewable penetration, and carbon intensity. A composite score makes the
weighting structure explicit and auditable, whereas a supervised model trained on external readiness labels
would impose implicit weightings that cannot be inspected. The weights are sourced from config.py, so
they can be updated for different policy priorities without retraining. The PCA validation step confirms
that the composite score captures a real latent factor rather than an arbitrary numerical average [src:
train_readiness.py, run()].

4.2
AQI Regressor (train_aqi_regressor.py)
The air quality module estimates the relationship between renewable capacity deployment and PM2.5
concentrations using a Gradient Boosted machine learning architecture.
4.2.1
Rationale
XGBoost with monotonic constraints is appropriate for this AQI regression task. The PM2.5 target exhibits
non-linear relationships with its predictors, particularly renewable capacity and industrial activity, and
XGBoost handles these interactions natively through gradient-boosted trees. The monotonic constraint on
renewable capacity ensures the model cannot predict decreasing PM2.5 as renewable capacity decreases,
even if the training dataset contains a spurious counter-example. Without this constraint, a model trained
on four states would have enough flexibility to learn noise from an 80-observation dataset and produce
physically implausible predictions.

5
Causal Analysis
Predictive modelling and causal inference answer different questions. A regression model identifies that
high renewable capacity correlates with lower PM2.5 concentrations; it cannot establish that increasing re-
newable capacity will cause PM2.5 to fall. The pipeline’s causal inference module causal_analysis.py
answers the interventional question directly: what would happen to regional air quality, groundwater
stress, and energy shortage risk if a policy deployed X MW of data centre capacity in state s [src:
causal_analysis.py, run()]?
The directed acyclic graph (DAG) is the foundational object of the causal framework. It encodes explicit
assumptions about which variables cause which, through which pathways, and which paths are blocked
by conditioning on observed confounders. Variable selection follows a mechanical procedure: identify all
backdoor paths from treatment to outcome, select a minimal adjustment set that blocks non-causal paths,
and condition only on that set. The assumptions encoded in the DAG are explicit and falsifiable through
conditional independence tests on observed data.
Orchestration note. analysis/causal_analysis.py is invoked as the final step of the standard
pipeline via run_all.py [src:
run_all.py, line 61]. Its outputs-causal_effects.csv,
did_results.csv, counterfactuals.csv, and causal_sensitivity.csv-are produced on
every full pipeline run provided data prerequisites are satisfied.

5.3
Sensitivity Analysis
1. Unmeasured confounding. The DAG cannot include variables that are not present in the dataset.
Industrial output, urbanisation rate, and infrastructure investment are plausible common causes of
both energy demand and air quality that are absent from the four-dataset corpus. Any such omitted
variable will partially confound the estimated treatment effects, biasing coefficients toward or away
from zero depending on the direction of the omitted correlation.
2. SUTVA violation. The stable unit treatment value assumption (SUTVA) requires that a state’s
potential outcomes are unaffected by which treatment other states receive. Indian state electricity
grids are interconnected through inter-state transmission agreements, and groundwater aquifers cross
administrative borders. A data centre added in Telangana may alter grid frequency in Andhra Pradesh
or reduce shared aquifer levels, violating SUTVA and rendering the estimated state-level effects
non-separable.
3. Absence of randomisation. Data centre siting decisions are not randomly assigned: states with better
infrastructure attract more investment, creating a selection-into-treatment bias. Without an instrument
or a natural experiment that induces exogenous variation in placement, the DiD estimate conflates the
causal effect of the data centre with pre-existing trends in high-readiness states. The parallel trends
diagnostic prescribed in Section ?? is a necessary but not sufficient check for this threat.

The deterministic model outputs answer what is most likely under current trajectories. They do not answer
how sensitive those outputs are to the assumptions embedded in the analysis, or what happens under
plausible-but-not-most-likely alternative futures. Two simulation modules address these gaps: the Monte
Carlo stability module quantifies sensitivity to subjective weighting choices, and the scenario simulation
module maps the space of plausible infrastructure futures.

Scenario Stress Simulation
Point forecasts predict the most likely outcome under a single set of assumptions. Infrastructure planning
requires understanding the range of plausible futures: what happens if data centre growth is faster than
expected, if agricultural water demand increases concurrently, or if renewable deployment falls behind
target? Scenario simulation maps this uncertainty space by computing groundwater extraction percentage
under a discrete grid of capacity multipliers. Each multiplier produces a conditional future, enabling
policymakers to identify tipping points: the minimum capacity at which a state crosses from a safe
operating regime into a critical or over-exploited one.

7.2
Textual Analysis and Comparative Sentiment
The integration of NLP models allows for a comparative analysis of public and industry sentiment across
the four study states. This layer captures qualitative signals—such as investment enthusiasm, regulatory
concerns, or community opposition—that are often missing from purely quantitative energy and water
datasets.
Key Finding: Sentiment Divergence. Lexicon-based models (VADER) consistently report higher
positive sentiment (µ ≈0.25) compared to transformer-based models (DistilBERT, µ ≈0.15). This
gap suggests that while the language of industry press releases is highly optimistic, the underlying
context captured by the transformer model reveals more neutral or cautious reporting regarding
infrastructure constraints [src:
textual_analysis/main.ipynb, ].
The analysis reveals that while most news coverage remains neutral to slightly positive (associated with
investment announcements), negative sentiment clusters around environmental impact reports and water
scarcity concerns in arid regions like Gujarat. Topic modelling via Latent Dirichlet Allocation (LDA)
identifies five recurring thematic clusters in the news corpus:
1. Infrastructure Expansion: Focused on hyperscale deployments and 5G grid readiness.
2. Institutional Investment: Tracking FDI inflows and private equity interest in the Indian data-centre
market.
3. Resource Stress: Centred on groundwater depletion and the PUE–WUE tradeoff in water-intensive
cooling.
4. Policy Frameworks: Coverage of state-level Data Centre Policies and renewable energy incentives.
5. Environmental Compliance: Reports on EIA clearances and sustainable building certifications
(LEED/IGBC).
These qualitative thematic frequencies provide early-warning signals for the quantitative risk tiers identified
in Section 6. For instance, a spike in “Resource Stress” topic frequency often precedes the Critical tier
crossing in groundwater scenario simulations by 6–12 months.

Strengths and Weaknesses Analysis
This section provides a rigorous assessment of the pipeline’s architectural strengths, identifies core scoped
limitations, and evaluates its external validity across different geographic and temporal contexts.
8.1
Architectural Strengths
Modular orchestration in run_all.py with per-step try/except isolation ensures a single module
failure does not corrupt downstream results. Centralised config.py makes the full parameter space
auditable in one file and separable from modelling logic. Three complementary uncertainty quantifi-
cation mechanisms, Monte Carlo weight perturbation, Bayesian Ridge posterior inference, and SHAP
attribution, characterise uncertainty across both the scoring and predictive layers. The DAG specification
in causal_analysis.py goes beyond the correlational analyses typical of comparable environmental
assessments by making confounding assumptions explicit and falsifiable.
8.2
Scoped Limitations
Intra-regional resolution. All supervised models train on state-level aggregates. The most-stressed
district within a state may face groundwater extraction rates two to three times the state mean, as the
district divergence analysis in Section 7 confirms. State-level readiness scores and impact thresholds
understate localised risk; the district-level groundwater regressor partially addresses this, but many
districts have fewer than five annual observations, making coefficient estimates indicative rather than
definitive for siting decisions in data-sparse areas.
Shortage classifier deactivated. Extreme class imbalance (97.8% negative-class rate across 6,134 train-
ing rows) prevents the binary shortage classifier from producing meaningful predictions. The pipeline

can model demand magnitude but cannot classify whether a shortage event will occur. This gap is
consequential for peak-stress planning, where the occurrence probability, not just the magnitude,
determines grid intervention timing.
DiD parallel trends untested. The difference-in-differences estimator in causal_analysis.py as-
sumes parallel pre-treatment trends between treated and control groups, but no pre-trend event study
is run [src:
causal_analysis.py, did_routine()]. Without this diagnostic, the extent to
which the estimated ˆβ3 reflects the data centre’s causal effect, rather than a pre-existing divergence
between high-readiness and low-readiness states, cannot be confirmed.
Training scope and national extrapolation. All supervised models are fitted on four states. The na-
tional ranking in Section 4.8 applies K-Means to extend readiness inference to all 36 Indian states and
union territories, including states that contributed no training observations. The cluster assignments
for out-of-sample states are structurally derived rather than model-validated; they should be treated as
indicative orderings rather than calibrated scores.
Dashboard data freshness. The Streamlit dashboard reads pre-computed artifacts at startup with no
embedded data vintage indicator. A policy analyst accessing the dashboard after a data update
has occurred but before the pipeline has been re-run will view stale outputs without notification.
Embedding a generation timestamp in each artifact header and surfacing it in the dashboard UI would
close this gap.
Textual Data Bias and Noise. The NLP pipeline in textual_analysis/main.ipynb relies on news
data harvested from public RSS feeds and NewsAPI. This data is inherently biased towards corporate
PR and investment announcements, which typically exhibit a strong “optimism bias.” Furthermore,
while deduplication was applied, the raw news corpus contains significant noise (e.g. duplicated
headlines from syndication) that can skew topic frequencies and sentiment means. Results from
this layer should be interpreted as media-perceived risk rather than direct on-ground risk [src:
textual_analysis/main.ipynb, ].
8.3
External Validity
The geographic scope is bounded by config.py [src:
config.py, STATES]: predictions for states
outside Telangana, Andhra Pradesh, Gujarat, and West Bengal require retraining on a broader dataset.
The forecast horizon of 2035 bounds temporal extrapolation; structural breaks in energy policy, monsoon
patterns, or agricultural water demand after that year are outside all model training windows.

Conclusion
This report documented and formalised an end-to-end environmental impact assessment pipeline for
AI data centre deployment in four Indian states. The pipeline integrates four independent government
datasets, air quality, groundwater, electricity, and renewable capacity, through a unified preprocessing
layer, trains seven model architectures across ten training modules, runs nine analysis modules, and
serves all outputs through a nine-tab Streamlit dashboard. Across approximately 35 CSV artifacts and 40
figure files, it produces readiness scores, environmental impact calculations, time-series forecasts, causal
effect estimates, Monte Carlo rank stability distributions, scenario stress simulations, and a long-horizon
